How to use Threadlands

Where to click, from finding your place to testing a plan. For the data and formulas behind the numbers, see How the calculations work →

Find your place

Search on the home page or open All places. Each place has its own page: today’s weather and news, key findings, what it depends on, and what’s changing. Not listed? Search for it in Explore and it builds itself in about ten minutes.

Read the map

On a place page, What’s here? shows heat, flooding or green space. Tap any spot to see what the data says about the area around it and the projects nearby. Start with a question, such as Where does the rain go from here?, to follow one thread.

Take part

Use Get involved on any place page: back a project, pin what you notice on the map, suggest a partner, or follow the place. Stand with tells others how you are connected to it. Reports and suggestions go to a private queue and are reviewed by hand.

Test changes in ThreadLab

In Explore, choose a goal such as cooling the hottest streets, or move the levers yourself. Switch the climate to 2050, compare the plan with doing nothing, and open the decision brief: a page you can print or save as a PDF to send. For places you follow, your last plan is kept on this device.

Design street by street in Studio

Studio lets you design a place element by element. From above, you paint whole hexes; zoom in to street level, over an OpenStreetMap street map, and you draw real shapes instead: a rectangle or outline for an urban farm, park, pond, rain garden, green roof, solar array or housing site, and a line for street trees, a transit route or a wildlife corridor. Each shape is measured in square meters or meters, priced from those quantities, and spread across the hexes it touches. Every element changes the same Threadlands model the Explore page uses:

  • Street trees, green roofs and urban farms add shade and greenery to a built-up hex, which lowers its heat load and adds a little rain storage. Farm plots and full urban farms also grow food. Urban farms yield about as much per square meter as field crops (Payen and others, 2022), and Studio counts people fed at about 0.19 ha of cropland per person. Redeveloping built land into a farm or park includes the cost of clearing it.
  • Rain gardens cut the flood exposure of the homes in their hex and add to the region’s storage.
  • Ponds, wetlands, forests and corridors change the land itself: they store floodwater, cool their neighbors and join up wildlife habitat.
  • Regenerative farming helps farmland hold water and survive drought.
  • Transit lines raise the low-carbon travel score. Infill housing makes room for more people in existing neighborhoods, so the farthest sprawl can stay unbuilt.
  • Solar roofs report clean power from the roof area and the place’s latitude.

At street level, Studio loads the real buildings, parking lots, vacant lots, parks and streets from OpenStreetMap, so you can click a real place to transform it, or draw with points that snap to real corners. The tilted view raises every building to its mapped height, or an estimate from its type and neighborhood where none is mapped. New buildings rise to the floors you set, adding homes; sea walls and levees rise to the height you set, shielding flood-prone land up to about 3 km behind them.

Everything exports for GIS. On Explore, Download GIS layers saves every hex of a place, with its land cover, people, elevation, heat, flooding and habitat, as one GeoJSON file that opens in QGIS or ArcGIS. In Studio, Download design for GIS saves every element you placed, with its size, cost and settings.

Things take time. Trees reach full canopy in about 15 years, wetlands mature in about 8, forests in about 20, and transit takes about 5 years to build. The year slider grows each element and warms the climate step by step toward the middle-path 2050 scenario, and every score is compared with doing nothing in the same year. Costs come from published US studies, adjusted to each country, and are listed with sources on the Studio page.
